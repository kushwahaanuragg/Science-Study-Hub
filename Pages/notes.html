Chapter 6: How Forces Affect Motion

1. Force

Force: A push or pull that can change an object's speed, direction, state of motion, or shape.

  • SI Unit: Newton (N)
  • Force is a vector quantity because it has both magnitude and direction.
  • Force can start or stop motion, change speed or direction, and change an object's shape.
2. Net Force

Net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on an object.

  • Forces in the same direction are added.
  • Forces in opposite directions are subtracted.
Balanced Forces

When the net force is zero, the forces are balanced. They do not change an object's velocity.

Unbalanced Forces

When the net force is non-zero, the forces are unbalanced and cause acceleration.

Remember: Zero net force does not mean zero velocity. An object can move with constant velocity.

3. Friction

Friction is a force that opposes relative motion, or the tendency of relative motion, between surfaces in contact.

Uses of Friction
  • Helps us walk.
  • Helps us grip objects and write.
  • Helps brakes stop vehicles.
Disadvantages of Friction
  • Causes wear and tear.
  • Produces unwanted heat.
  • Reduces machine efficiency.
Reducing Friction
  • Using lubricants.
  • Polishing surfaces.
  • Using ball bearings.
4. Newton's First Law of Motion

An object remains at rest or continues moving with uniform velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a non-zero net external force.

Inertia

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

  • Mass is a measure of inertia.
  • Greater mass means greater inertia.
  • Example: A passenger tends to move forward when a moving bus stops suddenly.
5. Newton's Second Law of Motion

The net external force equals the rate of change of momentum.

Momentum

Momentum is the product of an object's mass and velocity.

  • Formula: p = mv
  • SI Unit: kg m/s
Force and Acceleration

For an object of constant mass:

F = ma

  • F: Net force
  • m: Mass
  • a: Acceleration
  • Greater force produces greater acceleration for the same mass.
  • Greater mass produces smaller acceleration for the same force.
Weight

Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.

  • Formula: W = mg
  • W: Weight
  • m: Mass
  • g: Acceleration due to gravity
  • Near Earth's surface, g ≈ 9.8 m/s².
6. Newton's Third Law of Motion

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

  • Action and reaction forces act on different objects.
  • Therefore, they do not cancel each other on a single object.
  • Examples: Walking, swimming, jumping, and rocket propulsion.
7. System of Objects

A system is a group of objects considered together to study their motion.

  • Internal forces: Forces between objects within the system.
  • External forces: Forces exerted by objects outside the system.
  • Internal forces cancel in equal and opposite pairs when considering the complete system.
  • The net external force determines the change in the system's total momentum.
8. Important Formulas
  1. Net force in the same direction: Fnet = F₁ + F₂
  2. Net force in opposite directions: Fnet = |F₁ − F₂|
  3. Momentum: p = mv
  4. Newton's Second Law: Fnet = Δp / Δt
  5. Constant-mass form: Fnet = ma
  6. Acceleration: a = Fnet / m
  7. Weight: W = mg
  8. Force on a constant-mass system: Fexternal, net = M × aCM
9. Quick Revision
  • Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity.
  • Mass measures inertia; weight is a gravitational force.
  • Friction can be useful or harmful.
  • Action and reaction forces act on different objects.
  • Always use SI units in numerical problems.

Chapter 8: Journey Inside the Atom

1. Introduction to Atoms
  • All matter, living or non-living, is made up of atoms.
  • Atoms contain smaller particles called subatomic particles.
  • Scientific models of the atom have changed as new discoveries were made.
2. Development of Atomic Models
Dalton's Model
  • Matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms.
  • Dalton considered atoms indivisible.
Thomson's Model
  • The atom was described as a positively charged sphere with negatively charged electrons embedded in it.
  • It is often called the plum pudding model.
Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment
  • Alpha particles were directed at a thin sheet of gold foil.
  • Most particles passed straight through.
  • Some were deflected, and a very few bounced back.
Conclusions
  • Most of an atom is empty space.
  • Almost all its mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
  • Electrons occupy the space around the nucleus.
Bohr's Model
  • Electrons occupy specific energy levels or shells around the nucleus.
  • These shells are represented by K, L, M, N, and so on.
3. Subatomic Particles
  • Proton (p⁺): Positively charged; found in the nucleus.
  • Neutron (n⁰): Has no charge; found in the nucleus.
  • Electron (e⁻): Negatively charged; occupies the space around the nucleus.

Protons and neutrons contribute almost all the mass of an atom. The mass of an electron is much smaller.

4. Symbols of Elements
  • Each element has a chemical symbol.
  • Symbols usually contain one or two letters.
  • The first letter is always uppercase; the second, if present, is lowercase.
  • Examples: Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O), Sodium (Na), Iron (Fe), Potassium (K).
5. Atomic Number

The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus.

  • Atomic number = Number of protons.
  • In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons.
  • The atomic number identifies an element.
6. Mass Number

The mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in an atom's nucleus.

  • Mass number = Protons + Neutrons
  • Number of neutrons = A − Z

An atom is commonly represented as:

AZX

  • X = Symbol of the element
  • A = Mass number
  • Z = Atomic number
7. Distribution of Electrons

The arrangement of electrons in different shells is called electronic configuration.

Rules for Electron Distribution
  • Maximum electrons in a shell = 2n², where n is the shell number.
  • The K shell can hold up to 2 electrons.
  • The L shell can hold up to 8 electrons.
  • The outermost shell generally holds a maximum of 8 electrons in the introductory model.
  • For the first 18 elements, electrons are distributed among the shells in order, following these rules.
Examples
  • Hydrogen (1): 1
  • Helium (2): 2
  • Carbon (6): 2, 4
  • Oxygen (8): 2, 6
  • Sodium (11): 2, 8, 1
  • Magnesium (12): 2, 8, 2
  • Chlorine (17): 2, 8, 7
  • Argon (18): 2, 8, 8
8. Valency

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom.

  • Atoms may gain, lose, or share electrons when forming chemical bonds.
  • Atoms often combine to achieve a stable outer electron shell.
  • Valency depends on the electrons in the outermost shell.
Examples
  • Sodium (2, 8, 1): Valency = 1
  • Magnesium (2, 8, 2): Valency = 2
  • Oxygen (2, 6): Valency = 2
  • Carbon (2, 4): Valency = 4
  • Neon (2, 8): Valency = 0
9. Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

  • They have the same number of protons.
  • They have different numbers of neutrons.
  • Examples: Carbon-12 and Carbon-14; Hydrogen-1 and Hydrogen-2.
Uses of Isotopes
  • Uranium-235: Used as fuel in some nuclear reactors.
  • Cobalt-60: Used in certain cancer treatments.
  • Iodine-131: Used in certain thyroid treatments.
  • Carbon-14: Used to estimate the age of ancient organic remains.
Average Atomic Mass

An element's average atomic mass accounts for the masses and natural abundances of its isotopes.

Formula:

Average atomic mass = Σ (isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Example: If chlorine has 75% of its atoms with mass 35 u and 25% with mass 37 u:

Average atomic mass = (35 × 0.75) + (37 × 0.25)

= 35.5 u

10. Isobars

Isobars are atoms of different elements that have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

  • They belong to different elements.
  • They have different numbers of protons.
  • Examples: Argon-40, Potassium-40, and Calcium-40.
11. Important Points to Remember
  • Atomic number = Number of protons.
  • Mass number = Protons + Neutrons.
  • Neutrons = Mass number − Atomic number.
  • In a neutral atom, protons = electrons.
  • Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
  • Isobars have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
  • Valency describes an atom's combining capacity.

Chapter 9: Atomic Foundations of Matter

1. Law of Conservation of Mass

Matter can neither be created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction.

  • Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products.
  • This law was proposed by Antoine Lavoisier.
  • Example: If 12 g of carbon reacts with 32 g of oxygen, 44 g of carbon dioxide is formed.
2. Law of Constant Proportions

A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in a fixed proportion by mass.

  • This law is associated with Joseph Proust.
  • Water always contains hydrogen and oxygen in a mass ratio of 1:8.
  • The ratio remains the same regardless of the water's source.
3. Dalton's Atomic Theory

John Dalton proposed an atomic theory to explain the laws of chemical combination.

  • All matter is made up of atoms.
  • Atoms were considered indivisible in Dalton's model.
  • Atoms of the same element were considered identical in mass and properties in the original theory.
  • Atoms of different elements differ in their properties.
  • Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
  • Chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms.
4. How Atoms Combine

Atoms can lose, gain, or share electrons to achieve a stable outermost electron shell.

Ions

An ion is an atom or group of atoms carrying an electric charge.

  • Cation: A positively charged ion formed by losing electrons.
  • Anion: A negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons.
  • Examples: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻.
Covalent Bond

A covalent bond forms when atoms share electron pairs.

  • Single bond: One shared pair of electrons.
  • Double bond: Two shared pairs of electrons.
  • Examples: H₂, O₂, Cl₂, H₂O.
  • Covalent substances generally do not conduct electricity well.
Ionic Bond

An ionic bond forms due to the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

  • Electrons are transferred from one atom to another.
  • Positive and negative ions attract each other.
  • Example: Sodium chloride (NaCl).
  • Ionic compounds generally have high melting points.
  • They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water if their ions are free to move.
  • Solid ionic compounds generally do not conduct electricity because their ions cannot move freely.
5. Writing Chemical Formulae

A chemical formula represents the elements present in a substance and the number or simplest ratio of their atoms or ions.

Rules
  1. Write the symbol or formula of the positive ion first.
  2. Write the negative ion second.
  3. Choose subscripts so the total positive and negative charges balance.
  4. Reduce the subscripts to the simplest whole-number ratio.
  5. Do not write the subscript 1.
  6. Use brackets around a polyatomic ion when more than one of that ion is needed.
Examples
  • Sodium chloride: NaCl
  • Magnesium chloride: MgCl₂
  • Magnesium oxide: MgO
  • Calcium chloride: CaCl₂
  • Aluminium oxide: Al₂O₃
  • Sodium sulfate: Na₂SO₄
  • Calcium hydroxide: Ca(OH)₂
  • Ammonium nitrate: NH₄NO₃
6. Properties of Ionic Compounds
  • They contain positive and negative ions.
  • They are held together by strong electrostatic forces.
  • They generally have high melting and boiling points.
  • Many dissolve in water, although solubility varies.
  • They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved if mobile ions are present.
  • Example: Salt solution conducts electricity, while sugar solution generally does not.
7. Molecular Mass

Molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in one molecule.

Formula: Molecular mass = Sum of atomic masses of all atoms

Examples
  • H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = 18 u
  • CO₂ = 12 + 2(16) = 44 u
  • NH₃ = 14 + 3(1) = 17 u
  • CH₄ = 12 + 4(1) = 16 u
  • HNO₃ = 1 + 14 + 3(16) = 63 u
8. Formula Unit Mass

Formula unit mass is the sum of the atomic masses represented by the formula of an ionic compound.

It is used for ionic compounds, which consist of ions rather than separate molecules.

Examples
  • NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u
  • Na₂O = 2(23) + 16 = 62 u
  • MgCl₂ = 24 + 2(35.5) = 95 u
  • CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100 u
  • NaHCO₃ = 23 + 1 + 12 + 3(16) = 84 u
9. Quick Revision
  • Conservation of mass: Total mass of reactants equals total mass of products.
  • Constant proportions: A compound has a fixed composition by mass.
  • Covalent bond: Formed by sharing electrons.
  • Ionic bond: Attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • Cation: Positive ion.
  • Anion: Negative ion.
  • Molecular mass: Sum of atomic masses in a molecule.
  • Formula unit mass: Sum of atomic masses in the formula of an ionic compound.
  • Always balance ionic charges when writing chemical formulae.
